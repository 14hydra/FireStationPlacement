# Optimizing Fire Station Placement Based on Street Characteristics Using a Decision Tree Travel Time Model

ASI

## Overview

We predict how long a London fire engine takes to reach an incident from the characteristics of the streets it drives. We then use those predictions to decide where new fire stations should go. Team: Ricky Zhao and Sarp Akalin (ASI), targeting CSEF and possibly ISEF. We use London Fire Brigade (LFB) data, which records the station each engine actually left from, its turnout and driving times, and London's closure of 10 fire stations in 2014.

**Research question:** Does placing fire stations with travel times learned from street characteristics reduce real response times more than placing them by distance?

**Hypothesis:** Station sites chosen with a street-aware decision tree travel time model will give lower average and 90th-percentile response times on held-out 2025 London Fire Brigade calls than sites chosen by road distance or by the Kolesar travel time formula (Kolesar et al., 1975). Because travel times from a station that does not exist yet cannot be observed, we also test whether the model predicts a real change: the response times recorded after London closed 10 fire stations in 2014.

## Prior work

Fire station placement and fire engine travel times have been studied for about 50 years. Our project builds on five lines of work.

- **Fire engine travel time and distance.** Kolesar, Walker and Hausner (1975) found that a fire engine's travel time grows with the square root of distance on short trips, while it is still speeding up, and in a straight line on long trips. Kolesar and Blum (1973) showed that the average response distance in an area depends mainly on the area's size and the number of available engines. The Kolesar formula, refit on London trips, is our main baseline, and its prediction is also one of the travel time model's inputs.
- **Emergency vehicle travel time models.** Budge, Ingolfsson and Zerom (2010) modeled Calgary ambulance travel times from distance and showed that the spread of travel times also changes with distance. Westgate et al. (2013) estimated ambulance travel times along road routes with a Bayesian model. Poulton et al. (2018) studied how London ambulances move under blue lights. Urfalı and Eymen (2025) predicted fire response times in Kayseri, Turkey, with XGBoost on about 7,400 incidents (MAE 1.67 minutes, R² 0.46). These models use distance, time and location rather than the characteristics of the streets driven, and none of them uses the learned model to choose new station sites.
- **Learning street speeds from trip times.** Zhan, Hasan and Ukkusuri (2013) estimated travel times on each street from taxi trip records that give only the start, the end and the total time. Our learned fire truck speed per street segment applies the same idea to fire engines, which drive differently from taxis under lights and sirens.
- **Station placement models.** The set covering model (Toregas et al., 1971) finds the fewest stations that reach every area within a time limit. The maximal covering model (Church and ReVelle, 1974) reaches as many calls as possible with a fixed number of stations, which is our repositioning goal. The p-median model minimizes average travel time, and Larson's hypercube queuing model (1974) adds the chance that the nearest unit is busy. All of them need a travel time from every candidate site to every demand point, usually road distance at an assumed speed. Our contribution is to supply those travel times from a model trained on real fire responses and tested against real outcomes.
- **Station closures and their effects.** On 9 January 2014 London closed 10 fire stations: Belsize, Bow, Clerkenwell, Downham, Kingsland, Knightsbridge, Silvertown, Southwark, Westminster and Woolwich (London Fire Brigade, 2014). Taylor (2017) measured afterwards where response times got worse, using a spatial survival model. We use the same event as a test: whether a model trained only on calls from before the closures predicts the change that happened. Placement decisions have real consequences. That is why we test our travel times on real outcomes before trusting the placements they produce.

## Project plan

The project has three linked models. The travel time model predicts how long a trip takes, the route model is meant to predict which streets an engine will use, and the placement model uses the travel times to decide where firehouses should go. All three are built; the route model is a first version.

1. **Travel time model (built).** Predicts how long an engine takes to drive to an incident from the characteristics of the streets on its route and the conditions at the time. It is a gradient-boosted decision tree model (LightGBM), which adds up many small decision trees, each one correcting the errors of the trees before it, and averages four models trained with different random seeds. It predicts the full driving time directly. Established formulas enter as inputs rather than as a fixed part of the model: the Kolesar formula's prediction for the trip and the time of the fastest route at posted speed limits are two of its features, and the destination history feature measures how much slower or faster than the Kolesar formula past trips to the same area were. It is trained on London Fire Brigade trips without station IDs, so it can score sites that have no station yet.
2. **Route model (first version built).** Gives every street segment a fire engine speed learned from real trips and routes each trip along the quickest path under those speeds (see Route model below). The learned-speed time is also the base of a combined travel time model: LightGBM predicts only the correction to it. The combined model is as accurate as LightGBM alone (57.8 seconds MAE on 2025 trips for both, single seed), so it can replace it in placement (`--scorer lgbm_speeds`).
3. **Placement model (built).** Chooses firehouse locations on the street network. It combines the learned travel times with established operations research methods:
   - **Graph.** London's drive network from OpenStreetMap as a directed graph, so one-way streets are only driven the legal way. Every candidate site is routed to every demand area on it.
   - **Optimiser.** The p-median model as an integer program, solved exactly with HiGHS (with warm starts and a reported lower bound, so each answer is either proven optimal or comes with its distance from optimal). A genetic algorithm and a swap search are the fallback when the exact solver finds no layout in time.
   - **Busy engines.** The Erlang loss formula for each station's pumps, calibrated to the busy rates measured in LFB's records (see Novel aspects).
   - **Which station responds.** Layouts are scored with a learned dispatch model of which of the 8 fastest open stations sends the first engine (see Validation plan).
   - **Candidate sites.** Today's 102 stations, the 80 busiest 0.9 km demand areas, and the busiest area in every 3 km block. After the solve, each new site moves to the best junction on an A, B or local distributor road within 1 km. Junctions are shortlisted with a fastest-route estimate and then scored with the travel time model, and a move is kept only if it lowers the demand-weighted attendance time. This step moves 9 of the 10 new sites in the recommended move-up-to-10 layout by 170 to 760 m and saves 1.9 seconds on average; in the full redesign it moves 44 sites and saves 6.1 seconds. A site that no junction improves stays where it is and is named after its nearest street.
   - **Pumps.** Each station's number of pumps (fire engines) is a fixed input from LFB records (see Methodology). When a station moves, its pumps move with it. When stations are added, each new station takes the second pump of a nearby two-pump station, so London keeps its 142 pumps. A site is scored on when the first engine arrives, and the pump count decides how often a call goes to another station because every pump at the local station is out.
4. **Repositioning goal.** Given a fixed number of pumps, place stations to reach calls as quickly as possible, measured against London Fire Brigade's own attendance standards (below). Three experiments are compared with today's stations on held-out 2025 calls: adding 1, 2, 3 or 5 stations, moving up to 5 or 10 stations, and a full redesign of all 102. Demand is 2024 calls. Incident prediction, choosing how many pumps each station should have, and moving engines between stations during the day (dynamic cover moves) are left for future work; placement here is for fixed stations.
   - **Objective.** The optimiser minimises demand-weighted first-engine attendance time (predicted driving time plus the station's recorded turnout), in the style of the p-median model, so busy areas count for more. Three rules keep anyone from losing: the slowest 10% of calls may not be slower on average than today, the overall average may not be worse, and every area that today's stations reach within LFB's 10-minute standard must still be reached within it. The recommended layouts also require every borough's average to stay within 1 second of today.
   - **Weighting calls by severity (built).** About 60% of LFB engine trips are false alarms, so weighting every call the same lets automatic fire alarms in busy commercial areas pull stations toward them. LFB's records give each incident's type (fire, special service such as a road crash or a person trapped, or false alarm) and property type. No single set of weights is correct, so the optimiser runs under three: all calls equal (today's practice), fires and special services only, and fires in homes only (`--severity all|serious|dwelling_fire`). LFB withholds exact coordinates for calls to homes, so these use LFB's 50 m rounded grid reference; without it the planner had left out about 60% of calls, nearly all of them to homes.
   - **Time of day (built).** Stations cannot move during the day, so one layout is chosen for the whole day and then scored separately for night, both rush hours, daytime and evening (`scripts/placement_robustness.py`). Each 2025 call is scored with travel times predicted for its period and the hourly dispatch model. The recommended move-up-to-10 layout saves 4.6 to 5.6 seconds in every period, and the slowest 10% of calls are faster in every period. Adding 3 stations saves about 2 seconds in every period but makes the slowest 10% about 0.3 seconds slower at night and in rush hours, because the rule for the slowest calls is enforced over the whole day.
   - **Equal coverage against efficiency (built).** Weighting by demand favours the areas with the most calls, while an equal limit for every area treats a quiet suburb the same as a busy town centre. Instead of picking one compromise, a single dial sets the weighting: each area counts as (its weighted demand)^α. With α = 1 the optimiser is purely about efficiency (the p-median objective above); with α = 0 every area counts the same, so it is purely about equal coverage. `scripts/placement_tradeoff.py` runs α = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 and plots each layout's demand-weighted average time against its area average time, where every area with calls counts once. The placements form a trade-off curve (a Pareto front): no placement on it can improve one measure without worsening the other.
   - **How optimal a placement is.** We do not collapse efficiency and equal coverage into one weighted score, because the weight would be an arbitrary choice. Each layout gets two scores from 0 to 100: the share of the possible improvement it achieves on the call-weighted average, and on the area average, where 0 is today's stations and 100 is the proven best for that measure. With busy engines, the α = 1 layouts score 100 on efficiency but only 8 to 13 on equal coverage (a full redesign even scores below zero), while α = 0.5 layouts score 33 to 55 on efficiency and 55 to 86 on equal coverage. We chose α = 1 with the rules above: most of the efficiency gain, without any area, borough or the slowest calls losing.
   - **Placement for the slow calls (built).** LFB's standard is about slow calls, but the p-median model minimises the average. A second objective minimises the average of the slowest 10% of calls (CVaR), which keeps the problem linear, and a 90th-percentile LightGBM model (quantile loss) predicts slow-call times. Optimising the average barely changes the slowest calls (about 1 second); optimising the slowest 10% directly cuts them by about 12 seconds compared with today, at a cost of 2 seconds on the average (moving up to 10, engines assumed free). The recommended layouts instead keep the average objective and add the rule that the slowest 10% may not get worse.

## London attendance standards

We score placements against the standards London Fire Brigade sets for itself (London Fire Brigade, 2022), not US standards such as NFPA 1710. They apply to London as a whole, and LFB also tries to meet them in each borough.

- **First engine.** Arrives within an average of 6 minutes, and within 10 minutes on more than 90% of calls.
- **Second engine.** Arrives within an average of 8 minutes, and within 12 minutes on more than 95% of calls.
- **How the time is counted.** From the moment control mobilises the engine to its arrival, so it includes turnout as well as driving. Our travel time model predicts driving only, so when scoring a placement we add each station's recorded turnout time to the predicted driving time.

## Methodology

The first version of the travel time model is built and tested on London Fire Brigade data. These are the steps of that build, with the first results. Steps that are still planned say so.

1. **Data.** LFB mobilisation and incident records (Open Government Licence), which give the station each engine left from, its turnout time and its driving time. The current training set has about 1 million engine trips from 2021 to mid-2026. The 2014 closure test uses a second set of about 850,000 trips from 2009 to 2014. Supporting sources: fire station locations from OpenStreetMap, the OpenStreetMap drive network, Open-Meteo hourly weather (tested, not used), and ONS small-area income estimates for the equity check. The OS NGD road network, traffic counts and Google Maps travel times are planned (see London street data). Station size: resource codes are the station code plus a pump number, 1 or 2 (for example A211 and A212 for Paddington, station A21). For today's network, a station counts as two-pump when it often sends two of its own engines to one incident, which gives 40 two-pump and 62 one-pump stations (142 pumps). For 2013 and 2014 we count the distinct pump codes each station used regularly, which shows 26 stations whose pump count changed with the 2014 cuts. Cross-checking the counts against LFB's station pages is still to do.
2. **Where each engine started.** Each trip starts at the real building of the station the engine left from, as recorded by LFB. We keep trips where the engine left from a station (its own or another one), drop engines that were already out on the road and calls with a wrong address, and keep driving times between 30 seconds and 20 minutes. The median trip is 2.5 km of road and 4 minutes 25 seconds of driving, plus 71 seconds of turnout.
3. **Road graph and route.** Greater London's drive network from OpenStreetMap is a directed graph, so one-way streets can only be driven the legal way. For each trip we find the shortest legal route from the station to the incident and record its road distance, alongside the straight-line distance. This is the shortest route, not the route the engine actually drove; the route model (below) is meant to replace it.
4. **Street characteristics per route.** Along that route we compute the share on each road class (motorway or trunk, A, B, tertiary, residential, minor), the share on one-way streets, 20 mph streets, bus lanes and bridges or tunnels, the average number of lanes, and traffic signals, traffic calming, pedestrian crossings, give-way signs, junctions, turns, sharp turns and roundabouts per km. Carriageway width and other OS NGD fields are planned (see London street data).
5. **Context features.** Hour, day of week and month, rush hour and night flags, borough, and whether the engine came from outside the incident's station ground (a sign that the local engine was busy). Also the fastest route at posted speed limits, each end's distance to the nearest main road, the incident and property type, and destination history: how much slower or faster than the Kolesar formula past trips to the same 300 m and 1 km square were (computed only from training trips). Hourly weather (Open-Meteo) was tested and left out because it made the model slightly worse. Traffic counts and Google Maps civilian travel times are planned.
6. **Model.** Gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM, absolute-error loss) that predict the full driving time directly; the predictions of four models with different random seeds are averaged. The Kolesar prediction and the fastest route at posted speed limits are inputs, not a fixed formula the trees correct. Station IDs are left out so the model can score new sites. The target is driving time only, from leaving the station to arriving, which LFB records separately from turnout. LFB's attendance standards count from mobilisation, so placements are scored on predicted driving time plus each station's recorded turnout time. For a new site, whether the engine was busy has to be assumed, for example from a measured busy rate.
7. **Evaluation.** Train on 2021 to 2024 and test on 2025, so the test is fully in the future. Placement asks the model about places it has not seen, so we also test it on stations it never saw: the 102 stations are split into 20 groups, and each group's 2025 trips are predicted by a model trained without that group (see Validation plan).

**First results.** On 200,624 held-out trips in 2025, mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted driving time:

| Model | MAE |
|---|---|
| Constant guess | 111.9 s |
| Straight line at 32 km/h | 95.2 s |
| Road distance at 35 km/h | 80.8 s |
| Kolesar formula (straight line) | 71.4 s |
| Kolesar formula (road distance) | 68.7 s |
| LightGBM (distance and time) | 62.8 s |
| LightGBM with street features | 60.1 s |
| **LightGBM, all features, tuned, 4 seeds** | **57.6 s** |
| LightGBM, all features, on stations held out of training | 62.3 s |

On stations the model never saw, it loses about 4 seconds but still beats the Kolesar formula by about 6 seconds, at 98 of the 102 stations, so it carries over to new sites. Street features take 2.7 seconds (4%) off the error on top of road distance. The features that move predictions most are the share of 20 mph streets, traffic calming per km and turns per km. Removing all street features costs 2.4 seconds of MAE, but removing any one group costs at most 0.2 seconds, because the groups overlap: 20 mph streets are mostly residential, calmed and full of turns. On trips of at least 1 km, controlling for distance, hour and busy engines, routes on A roads, trunk roads, bus lanes and roads with more lanes go with a faster pace, and routes with more signals, 20 mph limits, one-way streets and traffic calming go with a slower one. These are associations, not causes: some features may stand in for the kind of area a route passes through, and OpenStreetMap under-tags bus lanes. The full analysis is in `docs/london_eda.md`.

**Direct prediction or a correction (built).** The current model predicts the whole driving time. The alternative is to predict only the correction to a simple road-distance estimate, such as road distance at a fixed speed or the Kolesar formula, and add it back. Decision trees cannot extrapolate: a tree model's prediction never goes beyond the range of times it saw in training, so a direct model may underpredict the longest trips, which matter when scoring new sites far from today's stations. A correction model inherits the road-distance estimate's growth with distance and only has to learn the departure from it. Built with learned street speeds as the base estimate (`scripts/train_street_speeds.py`; one seed each): LightGBM alone 57.8 seconds MAE on 2025 trips, street speeds plus a LightGBM correction 57.8 seconds, street speeds alone 65.9 seconds, the Kolesar formula 68.7 seconds. On trips over 6 km the correction model is about 1 second less accurate than LightGBM alone (104.5 against 103.3 seconds) but slightly less biased (−8.9 against −10.8 seconds), so on 2025 trips the expected advantage on long trips does not show; the test that matters for it is stations never seen in training.

**Further evaluation.** Built: the feature ladder above, an ablation of the street feature groups, SHAP and partial dependence plots (`docs/london_eda.md`), and a 90th-percentile quantile model for slow trips. Planned: comparison with a single decision tree, linear regression and a small neural net, and an ablation of traffic and Google Maps features once those are added.

## Novel aspects

These are what make the project new compared with the prior work above. Each says whether it is built or planned.

- **Street-level features for real emergency routes (built).** Each real London Fire Brigade response is linked to the road classes, lanes, speed limits, bus lanes, signals, traffic calming and turns of its street route from OpenStreetMap, not just its distance. On 2025 trips this lowers the error from 62.8 to 60.1 seconds (see Methodology). The route is still the shortest legal route; carriageway width from OS NGD and learned routes are planned.
- **Learned fire truck speed per street segment (first version built).** Each segment's time is its length times a pace for its road type, adjusted for a 20 mph limit, bus lane, one-way, two or more lanes and bridge or tunnel, plus a fixed time for each junction, traffic signal, traffic calming feature, pedestrian crossing, give-way sign and roundabout. With routes fixed the trip time is linear in these parameters, so they are fitted by robust regression on 701,000 trips from 2021 to 2024, and every trip is re-routed under the learned speeds and the fit repeated until the routes settle (3 rounds). Turns are left to the correction model, because a turn belongs to a pair of segments. The result is a map of how fast fire engines move on every London street (`data/figures/travel_time/street_speeds/speed_map.png`), and it scores any candidate site. Alone it predicts 2025 trips with 65.9 seconds MAE against 68.7 for the Kolesar formula. Zhan et al. (2013) estimated street speeds this way from taxi trips; we have not found it done for fire engines.
- **Placement for the slow calls (built).** London's standard requires the first engine within 10 minutes on more than 90% of calls, but placement methods usually minimise the average. We add a 90th-percentile travel time model and an objective for the slowest 10% of calls, and the recommended layouts may not make the slowest 10% worse (see Project plan).
- **Tested on a real closure (built).** Placement studies usually trust their travel time estimates without checking them on a real change. We test whether our model predicts the response times recorded after London's 2014 closures, and compare it with distance-based estimates (see Validation plan).
- **Busy engines and dispatch learned from real data (built).** When a station's engines are already out, LFB sends one from another station, which arrives later. Classic placement models, such as Larson's hypercube model (1974), take busy rates as inputs, usually one assumed value or a rate estimated from workload. We measure each station's rate from LFB's records: how often a call in its area was answered by another station in 2024. Each station's busy chance then comes from the Erlang loss formula for its pump count (a two-pump station only passes a call on when both pumps are out), calibrated to that measured rate and scaled with the calls it answers, so opening a station takes calls and busyness off its neighbours (`firestation.busy_placement`). The exact solver gives every area a primary and a backup station and re-solves as busy rates update. On top of this, a learned dispatch model predicts which of the 8 fastest open stations sends the first engine (`firestation.dispatch`), because LFB often sends a station other than the nearest free one. Results: today's modelled 2025 average rises from 284 seconds (engines always free) to 312 (busy engines) and 314 (dispatch model), against 332 recorded, so they explain over half the gap. Busy engines change which sites are picked (for moving 10 stations, 7 of the 10 new sites differ), and layouts chosen while ignoring them lose up to 15 seconds when scored with them.
- **Ranking the 10 closed stations (built).** Using the model trained only on calls from before 2014, we put each closed station back one at a time and predict, on 2025 calls, the seconds of average first-engine attendance time it would save and how many more calls would be reached within 6 and 10 minutes. The result is a ranked list of which closure costs London the most today. We check the ranking against the real change each closed station's area saw after January 2014. Taylor (2017) measured where response times worsened; we add a prediction of how much each station is worth and a check of that prediction against real data. First result: reopening any one of them in today's network would save 0.3 to 1.2 seconds of London-wide average attendance (Westminster most, Silvertown least); this ranking does not match the damage recorded in each area in 2014 (rank correlation −0.42), which is expected to be weak because demand and the network have changed since 2014 and the per-area 2014 effects are noisy.
- **Which street features slow fire engines (first version built).** The learned street speeds give each characteristic's effect directly, with bootstrap intervals (`docs/results.md`): learned speeds of 72 km/h on motorways and trunk roads, 69 on A roads, 62 on B roads, 57 on local distributors, 51 on residential and 41 on minor streets; a 20 mph limit adds 8.7 seconds per km and a one-way street 2.1, while a bus lane saves 2.4 and two or more lanes 3.2; each traffic calming feature adds about 1 second and each signal, crossing and junction about 0.6. Carriageway width (OS NGD) and traffic calming from the TfL Cycling Infrastructure Database are still to be added. This describes what the model learned, not cause and effect. A slow 20 mph street may be slow because of the limit or because it is narrow and lined with parked cars, and ordinary response data cannot tell these apart. So we do not use these numbers to predict what changing a street would do. Testing that would need a real street change, such as London's 2020 low-traffic neighbourhoods or its 20 mph rollouts, which we leave for future work.

## Validation plan

Response times from a station that does not exist yet cannot be observed, so a placement scored only by our own model would look good almost by definition. The planner's own score on 2025 calls uses 2025 call locations but the same travel time model that chose the placement, so it shows how well the placement fits the model, not that it would work on London's streets. These tests check the model against real outcomes. Every result table, and the list of figures, is kept in `docs/results.md`.

- **Real station closures (main test).** Train the travel time model on London Fire Brigade calls from before 9 January 2014, predict response times after the 10 closures, and compare with the times recorded in 2014. Do the same with road distance and the Kolesar formula. The 2014 cuts also removed pumps from stations that stayed open (the mobilisation records show 26 stations whose pump count changed), so the test uses each station's pump count for 2013 and 2014 from those records. The model that best predicts the real change is the one we use for placement. This differs from testing on ordinary incidents. An ordinary test only checks trips from stations that already exist, but placement asks what happens when the set of stations changes. After the closures, areas that lost their station were served from stations farther away, often along routes those crews rarely drove. That is the same kind of change our optimizer makes when it adds, removes or moves a station. Removing the 10 stations in the model and comparing its predictions with the times London actually recorded is the only test in this plan that checks that kind of change against real data. **First result:** models trained only on 2009–2013 trips all predict the London-wide effect of the closures (recorded +70.5 seconds in the closed stations' former areas, after removing London-wide trends) to within about 1–8 seconds: road-distance Kolesar +71.0, LightGBM +76.8, straight-line Kolesar +78.3. LightGBM is clearly the most accurate on individual post-closure trips (64.8 s MAE vs 73.4 and 74.8), but no model reliably ranks which of the 10 areas suffered most (correlations at most 0.29; each area has only a few hundred calls). So the test does not single out one model for placement; it supports using LightGBM for trip times while treating placement gains as uncertain by the spread between models. A follow-up diagnostic shows why: given the station that actually responded, the travel time models do predict which areas slowed most (correlation 0.88 for LightGBM, 0.84 and 0.75 for the Kolesar formulas), and LightGBM gets the overall drive change almost exactly (+60.0 against +61.8 seconds). The correlation falls to 0.42 only because placement assumes the nearest open station responds; after the closures the nearest station sent the first engine on as few as 38% of calls in some areas. So the next step is a dispatch model, learned from LFB's records, of which station responds, not a better travel time model. First version built: a decision-tree model of which of the 8 fastest open stations sends the first engine. On 2025 calls it reproduces how often the 1st, 2nd and 3rd nearest stations respond (72, 18 and 6% predicted against 70, 18 and 6% recorded) and halves the error of the nearest-station assumption per area (23 against 48 seconds). Trained only on 2009 to 2013, it raises the closure-test correlation across the 10 areas from 0.42 to 0.56; the rest is likely LFB's own repositioning of engines after the closures, which no record-based model of ordinary dispatch can see. A time-of-day version shows the nearest station is least available in the early afternoon (61% of first engines against 78 to 80% at night), when crews train and make fire-safety visits, rather than at the evening call peak; it reproduces that pattern but does not change all-day results, so it is for rush-hour and night comparisons.
- **New locations.** The accuracy that matters for scoring sites that have no station yet. Built (`scripts/new_station_check.py`) as leave-group-out cross-validation: 20 groups of about 5 stations, each held out once, so every station's 2025 trips are predicted by a model that never saw it (21 model fits instead of 103 for one station at a time). Result (all 102 stations, 200,624 trips from 2025): at stations it never saw, LightGBM's error rises from 58.3 to 62.3 seconds (median 3.6 seconds per station), still below the Kolesar road-distance formula (68.8 seconds) overall, at 98 of the 102 stations, and at every trip length, including trips over 6 km (109 against 126 seconds).
- **Several travel time models.** Score every placement (ours, distance-based and today's stations) under our model, the Kolesar formula on road distance and the Kolesar formula on straight-line distance. A placement counts as better only if it wins under all three. Built (`scripts/score_layouts.py`; the planner's `--scorer lgbm|road|kolesar`), with the learned dispatch model deciding which station responds: every layout chosen with LightGBM beats today's stations under all three models on 2025 calls. The recommended move-up-to-10 layout (every borough within 1 second) saves 5.5, 4.7 and 2.5 seconds on average and 3.8, 4.9 and 3.6 seconds on the slowest 10% of calls, all with 95% intervals below zero. The gain is largest under the model that chose the layout, so the spread between models is reported as the uncertainty on its size.
- **Statistics.** Built: paired bootstrap confidence intervals, resampling 2025 calls (1,000 times), on the difference between each layout and today in the average and in the average of the slowest 10% of calls.
- **Robustness.** The placement comparison repeated for each time of day, borough and severity weighting, and for different numbers of stations. Built (`scripts/placement_robustness.py`, `scripts/recommended_placement.py`). Every layout is faster for all calls, for fires and special services and for fires in homes, under all three models. Adding 1, 2, 3 or 5 stations saves 1.3, 1.4, 2.3 and 3.8 seconds with LightGBM (0.5 to 2.4 seconds under the straight-line formula). Moving up to 5 or 10 saves 2.3 and 5.5 seconds. Under LightGBM the borough rule holds except for the City of London (+2.6 seconds when moving 10, few calls); under the Kolesar formulas some boroughs get worse by up to 9 seconds (moving 10) and 12 seconds (full redesign), because the rule is enforced with the model that chose the layout.
- **Equity.** Who gains: the change in travel time by borough and by neighbourhood income. Built with ONS small-area income estimates (financial year ending 2020, household income after housing costs, about 980 London neighbourhoods split into fifths). Moving stations helps the poorest fifth most: 9.6 seconds when moving up to 10, against 2.8 to 5.6 seconds for the other fifths (LightGBM; the same order under both Kolesar formulas). Adding stations helps the richest fifth most (4.7 against 1.3 to 2.0 seconds), because the new sites are in central London.

## Route model (first version built)

The route model predicts which streets an engine drives from its firehouse to an incident. We do not assume engines take the shortest distance or the fastest route at posted speed limits; we assume they take the quickest route for an emergency vehicle, using a speed for each street learned from real emergency trips, because with lights and sirens on, drivers favour wide main roads and avoid narrow or blocked streets.

- **Road graph.** The directed OpenStreetMap street graph the project already uses (131,549 junctions), with the street characteristics computed for each segment.
- **Learned street speeds.** Each segment's time comes from its characteristics (see Novel aspects): a pace per road type, adjustments for 20 mph limits, bus lanes, one-way streets, lanes and bridges or tunnels, and fixed times for junctions, signals, traffic calming, crossings, give-way signs and roundabouts (`firestation.street_speeds`). Time of day and turns are left to the correction model.
- **Routing.** For each trip, the quickest path from the starting firehouse to the incident with Dijkstra's algorithm under the learned speeds (one search per station), adding up the segment times.
- **Training.** Robust regression of recorded driving times on the route sums, alternating with re-routing under the new speeds until the routes settle (`scripts/train_street_speeds.py`; 3 rounds, segment times changed 25%, 4% and 0.7%).
- **Validation.** No open dataset has GPS tracks of fire engine trips, and we are not requesting LFB's. So we test the routes through their travel times on held-out 2025 trips: learned-speed routes predict them with 65.9 seconds MAE, against 68.6 for the fastest route at posted speed limits (calibrated to drive times the same way) and 68.7 for the Kolesar formula on shortest-route distance; on trips over 6 km the gap to the speed-limit route is about 10 seconds (119 against 129). We claim only that the routes give accurate travel times, not that they match the routes engines drive. Still to do: test on stations never seen in training. The London Ambulance Service study by Poulton et al. (2018) gives a qualitative comparison of which roads blue-light vehicles favour.

## London street data (OS Data Hub)

London is our study city. Ordnance Survey's road network gives the street detail our features need. It comes from the OS National Geographic Database (OS NGD) through the OS Data Hub.

- **What it has.** For every London road segment: average and minimum carriageway width, bus lane and cycle lane presence, one-way direction, road type (single or dual carriageway, roundabout, slip road), tunnels, and the climb in each direction. OS also has turn, width and height restrictions, traffic calming such as speed bumps, and measured average speeds by time of day.
- **Gaps.** The lane count field is not filled in yet, and there is no parking lane data. Lane counts, speed limits and traffic signals come from OpenStreetMap, and TfL's free Cycling Infrastructure Database can cross-check cycle lanes and traffic calming.
- **Access.** Sign up for the OS Data Hub Premium plan and download Greater London's road links through the OS NGD API (Features). The plan includes £1,000 of free premium API use each month. London has a few hundred thousand road links, which should take a few thousand requests and fit inside the free allowance, but we will confirm the price per request on the Data Hub before downloading.
- **Fallback.** If the free allowance does not cover it, OS offers organisations a free three to six month Data Exploration Licence, and UK universities can get OS data through Digimap.
- **Terms.** Credit the data as "Contains OS data © Crown copyright and database rights", and check the Premium plan terms for how long downloaded data can be kept.
