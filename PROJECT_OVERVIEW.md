# Optimizing Fire Station Placement Based on Street Characteristics Using a Decision Tree Travel Time Model

ASI

## Overview

We predict how long a London fire engine takes to reach an incident from the characteristics of the streets it drives. We then use those predictions to decide where new fire stations should go. Team: Ricky Zhao and Sarp Akalin (ASI), targeting CSEF and possibly ISEF. We use London Fire Brigade (LFB) data, which records the station each engine actually left from, its turnout and driving times, and London's closure of 10 fire stations in 2014.

**Research question:** Does placing fire stations with travel times learned from street characteristics reduce real response times more than placing them by distance?

**Hypothesis:** Station sites chosen with a street-aware decision tree travel time model will give lower average and 90th-percentile response times on held-out 2025 London Fire Brigade calls than sites chosen by road distance or by the Kolesar travel time formula (Kolesar et al., 1975). Because travel times from a station that does not exist yet cannot be observed, we also test whether the model predicts a real change: the response times recorded after London closed 10 fire stations in 2014.

## Prior work

Fire station placement and fire engine travel times have been studied for about 50 years. Our project builds on five lines of work.

- **Fire engine travel time and distance.** Kolesar, Walker and Hausner (1975) found that a fire engine's travel time grows with the square root of distance on short trips, while it is still speeding up, and in a straight line on long trips. Kolesar and Blum (1973) showed that the average response distance in an area depends mainly on the area's size and the number of available engines. The Kolesar formula, refit on London trips, is our main baseline and the parametric part of our semi-parametric placement model.
- **Emergency vehicle travel time models.** Budge, Ingolfsson and Zerom (2010) modeled Calgary ambulance travel times from distance and showed that the spread of travel times also changes with distance. Westgate et al. (2013) estimated ambulance travel times along road routes with a Bayesian model. Poulton et al. (2018) studied how London ambulances move under blue lights. Urfalı and Eymen (2025) predicted fire response times in Kayseri, Turkey, with XGBoost on about 7,400 incidents (MAE 1.67 minutes, R² 0.46). These models use distance, time and location rather than the characteristics of the streets driven, and none of them uses the learned model to choose new station sites.
- **Learning street speeds from trip times.** Zhan, Hasan and Ukkusuri (2013) estimated travel times on each street from taxi trip records that give only the start, the end and the total time. Our learned fire truck speed per street segment applies the same idea to fire engines, which drive differently from taxis under lights and sirens.
- **Station placement models.** The set covering model (Toregas et al., 1971) finds the fewest stations that reach every area within a time limit. The maximal covering model (Church and ReVelle, 1974) reaches as many calls as possible with a fixed number of stations, which is our repositioning goal. The p-median model minimizes average travel time, and Larson's hypercube queuing model (1974) adds the chance that the nearest unit is busy. All of them need a travel time from every candidate site to every demand point, usually road distance at an assumed speed. Our contribution is to supply those travel times from a model trained on real fire responses and tested against real outcomes.
- **Station closures and their effects.** On 9 January 2014 London closed 10 fire stations: Belsize, Bow, Clerkenwell, Downham, Kingsland, Knightsbridge, Silvertown, Southwark, Westminster and Woolwich (London Fire Brigade, 2014). Taylor (2017) measured afterwards where response times got worse, using a spatial survival model. We use the same event as a test: whether a model trained only on calls from before the closures predicts the change that happened. Placement decisions have real consequences. That is why we test our travel times on real outcomes before trusting the placements they produce.

## Project plan

The project has three linked models. The travel time model predicts how long a trip takes, the route model predicts which streets an engine will use, and the placement model uses both to decide where firehouses should go.

1. **Travel time model.** Predicts how long an engine takes to reach an incident from the characteristics of the streets on its route and the conditions at the time. It is a gradient-boosted decision tree model (LightGBM), which adds up many small decision trees, each one correcting the errors of the trees before it. It predicts the full driving time directly, not a correction to a road-network estimate, and averages four models trained with different random seeds. It is trained on London Fire Brigade trips without station IDs, so it can score sites that have no station yet. It has no parametric part: the Kolesar formula is a baseline it is compared with, and the semi-parametric step is in the placement model.
2. **Route model.** Imitates the routes fire engines currently take, which are not always the shortest path. We will rebuild our route generation to match that behavior, then pass the predicted routes to the travel time model and the placement model.
3. **Placement model.** Chooses firehouse locations at a fine level of detail: individual streets, corners and one-way roads, not grid cells or neighborhoods. (First version built: the solver picks from station sites and 0.9 km demand cells, then each new site moves to the best junction on an A, B or local distributor road within 1 km, scored with the travel time model.) It combines graph theory, with the street network as a directed graph so one-way streets are respected, and a semi-parametric score for each candidate site: a parametric part for well-understood effects (the Kolesar distance formula refit on London trips, plus speed limits), and the travel time model's predictions for the rest. Each station's number of pumps (fire engines) is a fixed input taken from LFB records (see Methodology). When a station moves, its pumps move with it, and the total number of pumps in London stays the same. A site is scored on when the first engine arrives, and the pump count decides how often a call has to be answered by the next nearest station because every pump at the local station is already out (see Busy engines measured from real data).
   - **Street-level sites (planned).** Searching every junction in London at once is too slow, so the search runs in two stages. First the optimizer picks coarse grid areas for the stations. Then each chosen station is moved junction by junction along the street graph, and a move is kept only if it lowers total demand-weighted attendance time. We report how many seconds this street-level step gains over the grid answer.
4. **Repositioning goal.** Given a fixed number of firehouses, reposition them to reach as many fires as possible as quickly as possible, measured against London Fire Brigade's own attendance standards (below): average first-engine attendance time and the share of calls where the first engine arrives within 10 minutes. Demand is past incident density (2023–24 calls); incident prediction is left for future work, and so is choosing how many pumps each station should have. Moving engines between stations during the day to cover gaps (dynamic cover moves) is also left for future work; placement here is for fixed stations. A second experiment adds 1 to 3 new stations instead, and both are compared with today's locations on real 2025 calls.
   - **Objective.** The optimizer minimizes demand-weighted attendance time, in the style of the p-median model: each area's first-engine attendance time (predicted driving time plus the station's recorded turnout) is weighted by its demand, so busy urban areas count for more. This is subject to a maximum-time cap, so that no area is left beyond a set limit however few calls it has. The default cap is LFB's own standard: in every area, the first engine arrives within 10 minutes on 90% of calls.
   - **Weighting calls by severity (first version built).** Not every call needs a fast engine equally. About 60% of LFB engine trips are false alarms, so weighting every call the same lets automatic fire alarms in busy commercial areas pull stations toward them. LFB's records give each incident's type (fire, special service such as a road crash or a person trapped, or false alarm) and property type (dwelling, other residential, non-residential, outdoor, vehicle). We will weight demand by severity, with fires and special services counting more than false alarms and fires in homes counting most, because that is where delay costs lives. No single set of weights is correct, so we run the optimizer under several: all calls equal (today's practice), fires and special services only, and fires in homes only. We report whether the chosen sites change. A placement that holds up under every weighting is a stronger result than one that depends on it. (Built: `--severity all|serious|dwelling_fire`. LFB withholds exact coordinates for calls to homes, so these use LFB's 50 m rounded grid reference; without it the planner had left out about 60% of calls, nearly all of them to homes.)
   - **Time of day (planned).** Demand, travel times and busy rates all change by hour, so a layout is scored on its all-day expected time: each hour's attendance time, weighted by that hour's share of calls. We do not optimize a separate layout for each hour, since stations cannot move during the day. For the chosen layout we report rush hour and night separately, to show whether it holds up when the roads are busiest and when crews are fewest.
   - **Equal coverage against efficiency (first version built).** Weighting by demand favours the areas with the most calls, while an equal limit for every area treats a quiet suburb the same as a busy town centre. Instead of picking one compromise, we put a single dial on the weighting: each area counts as (its weighted demand)^α. With α = 1 the optimizer is purely about efficiency (the p-median objective above); with α = 0 every area counts the same, so it is purely about equal coverage. We run the optimizer at α = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 and plot each placement's demand-weighted average time against its area average time, where every area with calls counts once (and report the worst area and the share of areas missing the 10-minute standard). Built in `scripts/placement_tradeoff.py`. The α = 1 and α = 0 ends are each a proven optimum for their measure. The placements form a trade-off curve (a Pareto front): no placement on it can improve one measure without worsening the other.
   - **How optimal a placement is.** We do not collapse efficiency and equal coverage into one weighted score, because the weight would be an arbitrary choice. A placement's quality is its distance from the trade-off curve: if today's stations sit inside the curve, there are placements that are faster on average *and* fairer, and the gap in seconds on each measure says by how much. This gives one scale for comparing today's stations, our placement and the distance-based placements, without choosing a weight. In practice each layout gets two scores from 0 to 100: the share of the possible improvement it achieves on the call-weighted average, and on the area average, where 0 is today's stations and 100 is the proven best for that measure. First result (LightGBM travel times, 2024 demand, all calls): every layout from α = 0.25 to 1 beats today's stations on both measures. A full redesign at α = 0.5 scores 72 on efficiency and 68 on equal coverage, cutting the call-weighted average from 281.9 to 272.3 s and the area average from 324.8 to 310.4 s.
   - **Solvers.** The integer program (HiGHS) gives the proven optimum when the nearest engine is assumed free. A genetic algorithm, checked against it (same optimum or within 0.04%), optimises the busy-engine objective (1 − p)·t₁ + p·t₂, which the integer program cannot express linearly.

## London attendance standards

We score placements against the standards London Fire Brigade sets for itself (London Fire Brigade, 2022), not US standards such as NFPA 1710. They apply to London as a whole, and LFB also tries to meet them in each borough.

- **First engine.** Arrives within an average of 6 minutes, and within 10 minutes on more than 90% of calls.
- **Second engine.** Arrives within an average of 8 minutes, and within 12 minutes on more than 95% of calls.
- **How the time is counted.** From the moment control mobilises the engine to its arrival, so it includes turnout as well as driving. Our travel time model predicts driving only, so when scoring a placement we add each station's recorded turnout time to the predicted driving time.

## Methodology

The first version of the travel time model is built and tested on London Fire Brigade data. These are the steps of that build, with the first results. Steps that are still planned say so.

1. **Data.** LFB mobilisation and incident records (Open Government Licence), which give the station each engine left from, its turnout time and its driving time. The current training set has about 1 million engine trips from 2021 to mid-2026. Supporting sources: fire station locations from OpenStreetMap and the OpenStreetMap drive network, with the OS NGD road network to be added (see London street data). Open-Meteo hourly weather, traffic counts and Google Maps travel times are planned. Station size (planned): we count each station's pumps for each year from the same records, as the number of distinct pump resource codes that left that station as their home station. In the 2025 file every resource code is the station code plus a pump number, 1 or 2 (for example A211 and A212 for Paddington, station A21), and 103 stations send out pumps: 63 with one and 40 with two. We will check the format in the older years, cross-check the counts against LFB's station pages, and leave out specialist vehicles such as aerial ladders and rescue units if any appear; the 2025 file seems to hold only pumps.
2. **Where each engine started.** Each trip starts at the real building of the station the engine left from, as recorded by LFB. We keep trips where the engine left from a station (its own or another one), drop engines that were already out on the road and calls with a wrong address, and keep driving times between 30 seconds and 20 minutes. The median trip is 2.5 km of road and 4 minutes 25 seconds of driving, plus 71 seconds of turnout.
3. **Road graph and route.** Greater London's drive network from OpenStreetMap is a directed graph, so one-way streets can only be driven the legal way. For each trip we find the shortest legal route from the station to the incident and record its road distance, alongside the straight-line distance. This is the shortest route, not the route the engine actually drove; the route model (below) is meant to replace it.
4. **Street characteristics per route.** Along that route we compute the share on each road class (motorway or trunk, A, B, tertiary, residential, minor), the share on one-way streets, 20 mph streets, bus lanes and bridges or tunnels, the average number of lanes, and traffic signals, traffic calming, pedestrian crossings, give-way signs, junctions, turns, sharp turns and roundabouts per km. Carriageway width and other OS NGD fields are planned.
5. **Context features.** Hour, day of week and month, rush hour and night flags, borough, and whether the engine came from outside the incident's station ground (a sign that the local engine was busy). Also the fastest route at posted speed limits, each end's distance to the nearest main road, the incident and property type, and destination history: how much slower or faster than the Kolesar formula past trips to the same 300 m and 1 km square were (computed only from training trips). Hourly weather (Open-Meteo) was tested and left out because it made the model slightly worse. Traffic, recent call load, units sent and Google Maps civilian travel times are planned.
6. **Model.** Gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM, absolute-error loss) that predict the full driving time directly, with no parametric prior; the predictions of four models with different random seeds are averaged. Station IDs are left out so the model can score new sites. The target is driving time only, from leaving the station to arriving, which LFB records separately from turnout. LFB's attendance standards count from mobilisation, so placements are scored on predicted driving time plus each station's recorded turnout time. For a new site, whether the engine was busy has to be assumed, for example from a measured busy rate.
7. **Evaluation.** Train on 2021 to 2024 and test on 2025, so the test is fully in the future. Placement asks the model about places it has not seen, so we also test it on stations it never saw: the 102 stations are split into 5 groups, and each group's 2025 trips are predicted by a model trained without that group (see Validation plan).

**First results.** On 200,624 held-out trips in 2025, mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted driving time:

| Model | MAE |
|---|---|
| Constant guess | 111.9 s |
| Straight line at 32 km/h | 95.2 s |
| Road distance at 35 km/h | 80.8 s |
| Kolesar formula (straight line) | 71.4 s |
| Kolesar formula (road distance) | 68.7 s |
| LightGBM (distance and time) | 62.8 s |
| LightGBM with street features | 60.1 s |
| **LightGBM, all features, tuned, 4 seeds** | **57.6 s** |
| LightGBM, all features, on stations held out of training | 62.4 s |

On stations the model never saw, it loses about 5 seconds but still beats the Kolesar formula by about 6 seconds, so it carries over to new sites. Street features take 2.7 seconds (4%) off the error on top of road distance. The features that move predictions most are the share of 20 mph streets, traffic calming per km and turns per km. Removing all street features costs 2.4 seconds of MAE, but removing any one group costs at most 0.2 seconds, because the groups overlap: 20 mph streets are mostly residential, calmed and full of turns. On trips of at least 1 km, controlling for distance, hour and busy engines, routes on A roads, trunk roads, bus lanes and roads with more lanes go with a faster pace, and routes with more signals, 20 mph limits, one-way streets and traffic calming go with a slower one. These are associations, not causes: some features may stand in for the kind of area a route passes through, and OpenStreetMap under-tags bus lanes. The full analysis is in `docs/london_eda.md`.

**Direct prediction or a correction (planned comparison).** The model now predicts the whole driving time. The alternative is to predict only the correction to a simple road-distance estimate, such as road distance at a fixed speed or the Kolesar formula, and add it back. Decision trees cannot extrapolate: a tree model's prediction never goes beyond the range of times it saw in training, so a direct model may underpredict the longest trips, which matter when scoring new sites far from today's stations. A correction model inherits the road-distance estimate's growth with distance and only has to learn the departure from it. We will train both on 2021 to 2024 and compare them on all 2025 trips and on the longest 2025 trips, by MAE and bias, and keep whichever is more accurate on the long trips without losing accuracy overall.

**Further planned evaluation.** We will also compare the model with a single decision tree, linear regression and a small neural net. An ablation study removes one feature group at a time (street characteristics, traffic, weather, Google Maps) and reports the change in MAE, and SHAP or partial dependence plots show the effects of width, lanes, signals and turns. Quantile LightGBM models will give a prediction range for each trip.

## Novel aspects

These are what make the project new compared with the prior work above. The first is built; the rest are planned.

- **Street-level features for real emergency routes (first version built).** Each real London Fire Brigade response is linked to the road classes, lanes, speed limits, bus lanes, signals, traffic calming and turns of its street route from OpenStreetMap, not just its distance. On 2025 trips this lowers the error from 62.8 to 60.1 seconds (see Methodology). The route is still the shortest legal route; carriageway width from OS NGD and the learned routes are planned.
- **Learned fire truck speed per street segment (planned core).** We will fit a speed for every street segment from its characteristics so that the summed routes match recorded response times. That produces a map of how fast fire trucks really move on each street, and it can score any candidate station site, which a model that uses station IDs cannot. We check the fitted speeds against the 2025 test calls. Zhan et al. (2013) estimated street speeds this way from taxi trips; we have not found it done for fire engines.
- **Placement for the slow calls (planned).** London's standard requires the first engine within 10 minutes on more than 90% of calls, but placement methods usually minimize the average. The objective already reflects this through its cap: no area may fall below 90% of calls reached within 10 minutes. We will use quantile models to predict each area's slow-call times more accurately for that cap, and account for the local engine already being busy (see Busy engines measured from real data).
- **Tested on a real closure (planned core).** Placement studies usually trust their travel time estimates without checking them on a real change. We test whether our model predicts the response times recorded after London's 2014 closures, and compare it with distance-based estimates.
- **Busy engines measured from real data (first version built).** When a station's engine is already out on another call, LFB sends one from a different station, which arrives later. For every 2023–24 incident we compare the station ground the incident is in with the station the first engine actually came from, and count how often each station's own engine was unavailable, split by station, hour of day and season. Classic placement models, such as Larson's hypercube model (1974), take busy rates as inputs, usually as one assumed value or a rate estimated from workload. We feed each station's measured rate into placement, so a site's score includes the chance that its engine is busy and the extra time for the backup engine from the next nearest station. A station with two pumps only sends the call on when both are out, so we measure the rate separately for one-pump and two-pump stations and use each station's pump count to decide when a call spills over to the next nearest station. Stations also interact: a busier station spills more calls to its neighbours, which makes them busier in turn. So in place of the assumption that the nearest engine is always free, we use an availability formula, in the style of Larson's hypercube model, that works out each station's chance of being free from the calls it and its neighbours answer. Our model's average first-engine time is 287 seconds against 339 seconds recorded; we measure how much of that gap busy engines explain rather than assuming they explain all of it. We then test whether this predicts 2025 attendance times better than assuming the local engine is always free, and whether it changes which sites the optimizer picks. First version (`--use-busy`, `firestation.busy_placement`): each station's busy chance comes from the Erlang loss formula for its pump count, calibrated to its measured 2024 busy rate and scaled with the calls it answers, so opening a station takes calls and busyness off its neighbours. The exact solver gives every area a primary and a backup station and re-solves as busy rates update; the genetic algorithm optimises the full model directly. Results: modelled 2025 average for today's stations rises from 284 to 312 seconds against 332 recorded, so busy engines explain a little over half the gap. Busy engines change which sites are picked (for moving 10 stations, 7 of the 10 new sites differ), and they shrink the gains: a full redesign that looked 13 seconds faster assuming free engines is about 5 seconds faster with them, and layouts chosen while ignoring busy engines lose up to 15 seconds when scored with them.
- **Ranking the 10 closed stations (planned).** Using the model trained only on calls from before 2014, we put each closed station back one at a time and predict, on 2025 calls, the seconds of average first-engine attendance time it would save and how many more calls would be reached within 6 and 10 minutes. The result is a ranked list of which closure costs London the most today. We check the ranking against the real change each closed station's area saw after January 2014. Taylor (2017) measured where response times worsened; we add a prediction of how much each station is worth and a check of that prediction against real data.
- **Which street features slow fire engines (planned).** A first version, a pace regression on shortest routes, is in `docs/london_eda.md`. From the learned speed on each street segment, we will describe which street features go with slower or faster fire engine travel: traffic calming such as speed humps (TfL Cycling Infrastructure Database), bus lanes, carriageway width (OS NGD), 20 mph speed limits (OpenStreetMap), traffic signals and turns. We report a ranked table of seconds per kilometre with bootstrap confidence intervals and a map of London's slowest streets for fire engines. This describes what the model learned, not cause and effect. A slow 20 mph street may be slow because of the limit or because it is narrow and lined with parked cars, and ordinary response data cannot tell these apart. So we do not use these numbers to predict what changing a street would do. Testing that would need a real street change, such as London's 2020 low-traffic neighbourhoods or its 20 mph rollouts, which we leave for future work.

## Validation plan

Response times from a station that does not exist yet cannot be observed, so a placement scored only by our own model would look good almost by definition. The planner's own score on 2025 calls uses 2025 call locations but the same travel time model that chose the placement, so it shows how well the placement fits the model, not that it would work on London's streets. These tests check the model against real outcomes.

- **Real station closures (main test).** Train the travel time model on London Fire Brigade calls from before 9 January 2014, predict response times after the 10 closures, and compare with the times recorded in 2014. Do the same with road distance and the Kolesar formula. Some of the 2014 cuts removed pumps from stations that stayed open rather than closing whole stations (from general knowledge, not yet checked), so the test also takes away those pumps, using the pump counts per station for 2013 and 2014 from the mobilisation records. The model that best predicts the real change is the one we use for placement. This differs from testing on ordinary incidents. An ordinary test only checks trips from stations that already exist, but placement asks what happens when the set of stations changes. After the closures, areas that lost their station were served from stations farther away, often along routes those crews rarely drove. That is the same kind of change our optimizer makes when it adds, removes or moves a station. Removing the 10 stations in the model and comparing its predictions with the times London actually recorded is the only test in this plan that checks that kind of change against real data.
- **New locations.** Leave-one-firehouse-out cross-validation: train without one firehouse's trips, test on them, and repeat for every firehouse. This is the accuracy that matters for scoring sites that have no station yet, and we report it next to the time-split result.
- **Several travel time models.** Score every placement (ours, distance-based and today's stations) under our model, the Kolesar formula on road distance and the Kolesar formula on straight-line distance. A placement counts as better only if it wins under all three. The planner can already score with each of these (`--scorer lgbm`, `road`, `kolesar`).
- **Statistics.** Paired bootstrap confidence intervals, resampling calls, on the difference in average and 90th-percentile travel time between placements.
- **Robustness.** Repeat the placement comparison for rush hour and night, each borough and each severity weighting, and sweep over the number of new stations.
- **Equity.** Report who gains: the change in travel time by borough and by neighborhood median income.

## Route model (planned)

The route model predicts which streets an engine drives from its firehouse to an incident. We do not assume engines take the shortest distance or the fastest route at posted speed limits. We assume they take the quickest route for an emergency vehicle, using a speed for each street learned from real emergency trips, because with lights and sirens on, drivers favor wide main roads and avoid narrow or blocked streets.

- **Road graph.** Build a directed street graph from OpenStreetMap with osmnx, keeping one-way streets and the street characteristics we already compute for each segment.
- **Learned street speeds.** A model predicts each segment's emergency vehicle speed from its characteristics (width, lanes, road type, posted speed, bus lanes, signals, turns) and the time of day.
- **Routing.** For each trip, find the quickest path from the starting firehouse to the incident with Dijkstra's algorithm under the learned speeds, and add up the segment times.
- **Training.** Adjust the speed model until the summed route times match recorded travel times across the training trips, re-routing after each round because faster streets can change which route is quickest. The target is driving time only, from departure to arrival, so the time it takes crews to leave the station is excluded.
- **Validation data.** Testing route choice needs GPS tracks of real trips, and no open dataset has them. The options are a Freedom of Information request to the London Fire Brigade for engine GPS logs and the London Ambulance Service study "Modelling Metropolitan-area Ambulance Mobility under Blue Light Conditions", whose data is not public but whose findings we can compare with. We will file the request early in case it takes a while or needs narrowing. If no tracks arrive, we will claim only that our routes give accurate travel times, not that they match the routes engines drive.
- **Validation method.** Map-match each GPS track onto the road graph with Valhalla or the leuvenmapmatching package, route the same trip with our model, and measure route overlap: the share of the real route's length that our route also uses, plus how often the two routes are identical.
- **Baselines.** Score the shortest-distance route and the fastest route at posted speed limits the same way. The route model is only useful if it matches real routes more closely than both, since it is meant to copy real routing, not improve on it.
- **Check without GPS tracks.** If our routes' summed times match held-out recorded travel times, the routes are plausible, but that does not prove engines drove them.

## London street data (OS Data Hub)

London is our study city. Ordnance Survey's road network gives the street detail our features need. It comes from the OS National Geographic Database (OS NGD) through the OS Data Hub.

- **What it has.** For every London road segment: average and minimum carriageway width, bus lane and cycle lane presence, one-way direction, road type (single or dual carriageway, roundabout, slip road), tunnels, and the climb in each direction. OS also has turn, width and height restrictions, traffic calming such as speed bumps, and measured average speeds by time of day.
- **Gaps.** The lane count field is not filled in yet, and there is no parking lane data. Lane counts, speed limits and traffic signals come from OpenStreetMap, and TfL's free Cycling Infrastructure Database can cross-check cycle lanes and traffic calming.
- **Access.** Sign up for the OS Data Hub Premium plan and download Greater London's road links through the OS NGD API (Features). The plan includes £1,000 of free premium API use each month. London has a few hundred thousand road links, which should take a few thousand requests and fit inside the free allowance, but we will confirm the price per request on the Data Hub before downloading.
- **Fallback.** If the free allowance does not cover it, OS offers organisations a free three to six month Data Exploration Licence, and UK universities can get OS data through Digimap.
- **Terms.** Credit the data as "Contains OS data © Crown copyright and database rights", and check the Premium plan terms for how long downloaded data can be kept.
